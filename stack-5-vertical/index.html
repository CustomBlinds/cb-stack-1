Vertical Blinds for Sliding Doors in South Africa

The sliding door is the most common problem opening in a South African home, and the one most often covered badly. It is wide, it is glazed from floor to ceiling, it faces the garden, and it is the door everybody actually walks through. Cover it with the wrong thing and you get one of two outcomes: a blind that never fully clears the doorway, or a curtain that gets pushed aside every time someone carries a tray outside and stays that way.

Vertical blinds exist for exactly this surface. They are the one system in the indoor range designed around width rather than drop, and around a doorway that has to keep working as a doorway.

Why Width Changes the Engineering

Most indoor blinds are engineered for a window: roughly as tall as they are wide, opening upward, stacking at the top. A sliding door reverses that. It can run three metres or more across, and the covering has to move sideways and get completely out of the way.

A vertical blind handles that by hanging louvres from a track rather than rolling fabric onto a tube. The weight is carried across the whole track instead of concentrated on one roller, which is why a single vertical blind can span an opening that would need two or three roller blinds side by side. It also means no seams down the middle of your view, and no gaps between separate blinds where afternoon light comes through.

Stack Position Is the Decision Most People Miss

When vertical louvres are drawn back they gather into a stack, and where that stack sits is a genuine design decision rather than a detail. Stack it on the wrong side and it sits permanently in front of the half of the door that opens. Stack it split to both sides and you halve the bunching but lose a little glass at each end.

The right answer depends on which panel of your door actually slides, which way the room is laid out, and whether the door leads to a patio you use daily or a garden you look at more than you walk into. This is worth deciding before ordering, not after, because it is set by the track and cannot be changed later without replacing it.

Light, Heat and the Angle Nobody Talks About

Louvres rotate, and that rotation is the real advantage on a glazed door. A west-facing sliding door in a South African summer takes direct low sun for the last hours of the afternoon, which is precisely when the room is in use. Rotating the louvres a few degrees deflects that low sun off the glass while leaving the view largely open, something a roller blind cannot do without covering the door entirely.

The same rotation works for privacy at night. Angle the louvres and the room reads as closed from the garden while the occupants can still see out, which matters on a ground-floor door facing a shared boundary.

What to Check Before You Order

Measure the full opening including the frame, not just the glass, and measure the height at both ends. Sliding door frames are frequently a few millimetres out of square, and a vertical blind cut to the shorter measurement will hang clear of the floor at one end.

Check the track length against the wall you have, not just the door. A vertical blind can be tracked wider than the opening so the stack clears the glass completely, but only if there is wall to take it.

Then specify the louvre width to the room, and choose the fabric for the aspect. These are the details that separate a blind that works on a door from one that merely covers it, and they are set out for the South African range on this guide to sizing vertical blinds for sliding doors from Custom Blinds Shutters & Awnings, an independent Garden Route company trading since 2010.

Made to the Opening, Not to a Standard Size

Standard-size blinds and a sliding door rarely meet in the middle. The opening is whatever the builder made it, and the covering has to be cut to that. Made-to-measure is not an upgrade on this surface so much as the baseline requirement, and it is the reason a sliding door is worth measuring properly once rather than approximating twice.
